        Game of AGI

        Origins and sources

        This version extends the original Game of AGI, a Monte Carlo of AGI risk driven by safe-by-default odds, safety spending, oversight, the number of labs that originate AGI, whistleblowers, and takeoff vs. replication time.

        The main sources: AI 2040: Plan A for timelines and the plans' odds of getting alignment right; a separate factored Monte Carlo estimate for open-weights pandemic risk; and Superintelligence Strategy (Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang) for the deterrence argument (MAIM) behind leaving state programs out; and experts on Chinese AI policy (Concordia AI, Matt Sheehan at Carnegie, SemiAnalysis) for how much alignment work China does itself and how readily it would slow down; and Craig Quiter's National Security Response for how automation can leave most people without economic leverage or a say even when humans keep control of AI (Locked-In Power and the public wealth fund); and Gladstone AI's report on recursive self-improvement (covered by TIME) for the RSI 2027 scenario, where lab insiders expect AI to start substantially speeding up AI research during 2027.

        The code is open source on GitHub (MIT license).

          How the model works

          Game of AGI is a yearly Monte Carlo model of 2027–2040. Every equation and constant below is the one model.js runs. Tags show where each number comes from: AI 2040 is taken from or calibrated to AI 2040: Plan A, Bio decomposition is calibrated to a separate factored Monte Carlo estimate of bio risk, Informed by … is a judgment informed by the published evidence it names, which does not measure the quantity directly, Consistent with … or Within … marks a judgment checked against measured anchors, and Assumption is a judgment call in this model.

          1. One simulated year

          1. Compute this year's three catastrophe hazards from the current state (section 3).
          2. Roll for each hazard in turn, each with its own die: a pandemic with probability P_{\text{bio}}, then loss of control with P_{\text{loc}}, then great-power war with P_{\text{war}}. The first hit ends the future, so the three combine as independent risks.
          3. Otherwise apply the yearly updates (section 5), clamp every state variable to 0–100 and move to the next year. Every update reads the state as it stood at the start of the year, so the order of the updates does not matter. The one exception is a deal shock (section 5): when a deal dissolves or weakens, the cut to coordination K applies this year, before alignment's sharing term and coordination's own update read it.
          4. If frontier capability reaches 100, resolve the superintelligence handoff; in 2040, resolve the ending (section 4).

          The Futures Lab runs 2,000 futures with the levers held fixed. In the campaign, each decision card also moves levers and state directly (events.js).

The risk panel shows risks averaged over them; the dice use the hidden draw. \mathcal{N}(\mu,\ \sigma) is a normal; \mathcal{B}(\mu,\ \sigma) is a Beta distribution on [0, 1] (or the range given) with mean \mu and standard deviation \sigma, so a probability stays between 0 and 100% without piling up at the ends. Its shape parameters follow from the two by the method of moments, after rescaling the range [lo, hi] to [0, 1]: with m = (μ − lo)/(hi − lo) and s = σ/(hi − lo), \alpha = m k and \beta = (1-m)\,k with k = m(1−m)/s² − 1, and the draw is scaled back to [lo, hi]. The spread is capped at s = 0.95 × √(m(1−m)), just under the largest a Beta allows, and a spread of 0 fixes the value at its mean. Until October 2026 these probabilities were normals clipped to [0, 1]; the Betas keep those curves' actual means and spreads (kindness was labelled 10% ± 25 but averaged about 16%, with 35% of worlds at exactly 0).

          3. Annual hazards

          Engineered pandemic (100M+ deaths). This model counts only pandemics enabled by open-weights AI. Natural outbreaks and state programs (c.f. MAIM) are not included:

          P_{\text{bio}} = 1 - e^{-k\,g}, \qquad g = \sigma\big(\tfrac{O-⟦bioOpenMid⟧}{⟦bioOpenW⟧}\big)\Big(1-\tfrac{B}{100}\Big)^{⟦bioDefExp⟧}
          k = \frac{-\ln\left(1-R_{\text{bio}}\right)}{G_{\text{ref}}}, \qquad G_{\text{ref}} = \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=2027}^{\min(2036,\ \text{end})} g_t\Big]_{\text{default path}} \approx {{GREF}}

          g is uplift from open weights, which matters once open models pass expert level, times missing biodefense. G_{\text{ref}} is the exposure that default-path futures actually go through from 2027 to 2036, summed until each future ends (most end at the superintelligence handoff around 2031), averaged over 3,000 fixed-seed futures. So the default path is calibrated to the target distribution on average (the median matches); an individual future's realized risk still varies with its own exposure, and policy moves risk through g. Paths that last longer with open models near the frontier face more risk: Plan A with fully open weights reaches about 4.3%, cut to about 1.6% by full biodefense (80,000 futures). Because g is steep where default-path open models sit, G_{\text{ref}} is sensitive to how open models grow: when they began chasing the start-of-year frontier rather than the end-of-year one, G_{\text{ref}} fell from 0.19 to 0.06, roughly tripling k and raising the pandemic risk of paths that last longer than the default (not by a single common factor: Plan A with open weights went from about 2.3% to 4.3%). G_{\text{ref}} is computed under the default assumptions; when the Lab changes progress speed or ease of alignment, risk scales with the exposure those futures go through relative to that baseline. Bio decomposition The thresholds are Assumption. The risk panel shows the expected probability over the unknown R_{\text{bio}}, \mathbb{E}\big[1-e^{-k g}\big], which rare bad worlds dominate.

          Why the pandemic risk is so low. An earlier version of this model used a rough guess that put open-weights pandemic risk near 1.7% a year, about 21% over 2027–2040. The factored Monte Carlo estimate in the R_{\text{bio}} row above came out about 1,300 times lower, so the model now follows it. That estimate counts only pandemics that kill at least 100 million people and would not have happened without open weights, and every step from model access to a global catastrophe has to succeed. Multiplying those step probabilities gives a median of about 1 in 8,000 per decade. The model treats other AI-enabled pandemics as negligible: states able to build such weapons are deterred from using them, much as Superintelligence Strategy (Hendrycks, Schmidt and Wang) argues states deter each other's dangerous AI projects, and closed models keep capabilities away from the rogue actors the paper identifies as the main bioterror risk. The tail stays heavy: about a 5.5% chance the true decade risk is above 1%, and 0.7% that it is above 10%. You can widen or shift that distribution in the Futures Lab. Bio decomposition

          4. Endings

          Superintelligence handoff. When C reaches 100:

          \Pr(\text{handoff goes well}) = \begin{cases} 1 & \delta \le ⟦diffSafe⟧ \\ \sigma\big(\tfrac{\hat{A}-\beta}{⟦barW⟧}\big) & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}, \qquad \beta = ⟦barBase⟧ + ⟦barDiff⟧\,\delta + ⟦barInternal⟧\,(H-⟦internal0⟧) + ⟦barDeleg⟧\,(\Lambda-⟦deleg0⟧) - ⟦barBci⟧\,\tfrac{N}{100}

          The bar \beta rises with hidden difficulty, with hidden internal AI and with delegation \Lambda (a society that has handed most decisions to AI is less able to step back in: 3 points at \Lambda = 32), and falls when BCIs let humans check AI reasoning directly. This is a hypothetical mechanism: today's implants are assistive devices used by about 20 people at around 10 bits per second, and none has been shown to help anyone understand or oversee AI. A failed handoff is loss of control, which ends as a Benevolent Takeover if W = 1. A successful one still ends in Locked-In Power, a catastrophe, with probability

          A few end up holding AI and nearly all its gains while most people lose their economic leverage and their say, whether power was seized or drifted there as automation moved value from labor to whoever owns AI. The baseline pressure scales with 1-\psi, the chance the few do not share the gains when nothing forces them; at the default \psi of about 0.3 that is 1.7.           The deal. A verified US–China deal is in force when K \ge ⟦dealCoord⟧ and d \ge ⟦dealDip⟧ and no deal has collapsed. It holds both blocs at 78, top-human-expert level, like Plan A's 2035 pause (a bloc already above 78 is frozen where it is). Campaign choices made while it holds cannot push capability past that cap. Deals decline in two ways, following AI 2040's Deal Decline supplement, each with the same yearly hazard by deal age (counted from the first year the deal is in force): 6% in its first two years, 3.5% in years 2–5 and 2% after that. Dissolution cuts K by 40 and the deal never returns: about 28% of deals dissolve within ten years. Impairment happens at most once: it cuts K by 20 and raises the ceiling from 78 to 84, and the deal stays in force as long as K \ge ⟦dealCoord⟧. Together about 48% of deals dissolve or weaken within ten years AI 2040. The deal still holds through the year it dissolves or weakens, so the change bites the following year. The size of the impairment's effects is an Assumption; the model has no separate verification variable to weaken.

          8. What it leaves out

          Covert projects and cheating on the deal (beyond the age-based dissolution and impairment rates), cyberattacks, countries other than the two blocs, misuse beyond bio, conflict between AGIs, anything after 2040, plans that change over time in the Futures Lab, and whether capability can really be put on one axis at all. Treat it as an argument you can poke at, not an oracle.
